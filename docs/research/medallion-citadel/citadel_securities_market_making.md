## Revenue and scale (2020–2026), market share, options, Treasuries/FX

### Takeaway
Citadel Securities' net trading revenue went from about US$7.5bn (2022) and about US$6.3bn (2023) to US$9.7bn (2024) and a record US$12.2bn (2025). Q1 2026 was a further record at US$4.3bn. ### Inferences
- **(c) Retail internalisation is small in the revenue mix.** The SEC's Table 5 (Q1 2022, Rule 605 "covered" orders) gives wholesaler marketable-order dollar volume of US$5,122.9bn and a realised half-spread of 0.61 bps. The SEC itself warns that realised spread is "an imprecise proxy" for profit: it ignores PFOF, inventory exit prices and fixed costs ([34-96495 fn 426](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf)). Rule 605 also excludes orders of 10,000 shares or more and non-regular-hours trading.
- **(c) Revenue tracks volatility and volume, not a fixed fee stream.** The record years (2020, 2022, 2025, Q1 2026) line up with volatility episodes, and 2023 was the dip. For the gap analysis this matters because the market maker's edge is a share of total turnover times spread, adjusted for adverse selection. It is not a forecast of direction.

## The mechanism: spread capture, inventory, adverse selection, why retail flow is valuable, PFOF and internalisation, technology

### Takeaway
A market maker earns the bid-ask spread and loses to traders who know more than it does (adverse selection). Wholesale retail market making works because flow sorted out as retail ("segmented" flow) has about 71% lower adverse selection than exchange flow. That makes it cheap to fill, so wholesalers can give price improvement, pay PFOF of about $0.001–0.003 per share, and still earn a positive realised spread. Exchange liquidity providers earn a negative one on average. The edge is the ability to identify uninformed flow and to predict and hedge the next few minutes. It is not the spread alone.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory (standard references, not re-read in this session):**
  - Glosten & Milgrom (1985): the spread exists even with zero processing costs because the dealer loses to informed traders and must recoup that from uninformed ones ([JFE 14(1)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(85)90044-3)).
  - Kyle (1985): price impact (λ) rises with the share of informed trading and falls with noise-trader volume ([Econometrica 53(6)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913210)).
  - Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008): the optimal market maker quotes around an inventory-adjusted "reservation price". It skews quotes to shed inventory, with spread width set by risk aversion, volatility and order-arrival intensity ([Quantitative Finance 8(3)](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697680701381228)).
  - **(a)** These are canonical. I did not fetch the full texts this session.
- **Empirical HFT market maker (Menkveld 2013, JFM):** "The HFT incurs a loss on its inventory but earns a profit on the bid-ask spread." It participated in 8.1% of trades on the incumbent market (Euronext) and 64.4% on the entrant (Chi-X). About four out of five of its trades were passive. Its Sharpe ratio was very sensitive to cost-of-capital assumptions. **(a/b)** Abstract content via search snippet ([SSRN 1722924](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1722924); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:16:y:2013:i:4:p:712-740)). The page itself returned 403.
- **Why retail flow is valuable (SEC, primary):** individual-investor flow is segmented because it is "low-cost" flow that imposes lower adverse selection costs. Adverse selection, measured as price impact, is **1.26 bps at wholesalers vs 4.40 bps on exchanges, 71% lower** (Table 7). **(a)** [SEC 34-96495 pp. 7–8, fn 6](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf).
- **SEC Table 5 (Q1 2022, NMS common stocks + ETFs, marketable orders), wholesalers (WH) vs exchanges (EX)** **(a)** [same release, p. 214](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf):

  | Metric | WH | EX |
  |---|---|---|
  | Effective half-spread | 1.81 bps | 2.06 bps |
  | Realised half-spread (5-min) | +0.61 bps | −0.38 bps |
  | Price impact | 1.20 bps | 2.44 bps |
  | E/Q ratio (effective ÷ quoted spread) | 0.48 | 1.01 |
  | Shares price-improved | 83.17% | 8.78% |
  | Fill rate | 69.32% | 25.77% |

  - Market orders alone at wholesalers: E/Q 0.40, realised spread 0.39 bps, average conditional price improvement 2.33 bps.
  - About 79% of the dollar volume wholesalers handle is market orders.
- **The competitive shortfall argument:** the SEC estimated retail orders were short-changed by about **1.08 bps per dollar traded, about US$1.5bn a year**, because of no order-by-order competition. Its argument: higher wholesaler realised spreads suggest higher economic profit than in competitive venues. **(a)** [SEC 34-96495 p. 10, p. 212](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf). This is contested. Critics argue realised spread is revenue after adverse selection, not profit ([Cato, Regulation Winter 2023–24](https://www.cato.org/regulation/winter-2023-2024/examining-secs-proposed-order-competition-rule), snippet) **(b)**.
- **PFOF totals (SEC, primary):** the six largest wholesalers paid **US$235m of PFOF in Q1 2022** for NMS stocks: US$94m on market orders, US$53m on marketable limit, US$69m on non-marketable limit and US$19m other. Brokers that accept PFOF account for 73.88% of the marketable dollar volume routed to wholesalers. One broker received more than 43% of all PFOF, or about 55% once merged with the number-four recipient (a 2020 merger). The top four received about 94%. The largest recipient got US$101.5m, equal to 5.7% of its total revenue. **(a)** [SEC 34-96495 p. 6, pp. 244–245, fn 465, fn 611](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf). The SEC does not name the brokers. The 2020 merger description fits Schwab/TD Ameritrade **(c)**.
- **PFOF per-share rates (Rule 606 reports):**
  - Citadel Securities pays one broker **up to $0.0022/share for marketable orders** and $0.003 for non-marketable orders, $0.001 extended hours, and $0.003 for orders over 10,000 shares or $250k (Q2 2024) ([FINRA-hosted 606 report, broker 136131](https://cdn.finra.org/606/2024/136131_606_NMS_2024_Q2_V1.pdf)) **(a/b)**, snippet; the broker is not identified.
  - TradeStation, Q3 2024: **$0.0026 marketable / $0.0030 non-marketable** from Citadel ([TradeStation 606](https://cdn.tradestation.com/uploads/2024-Q3-SEC-Rule-606-607.pdf)) **(a/b)**, snippet.
  - Schwarz et al. report PFOF of about $0.001/share at TD Ameritrade/Schwab and about $0.002 at E*Trade/Robinhood, roughly the same across venues for each broker ([Schwarz et al. WP, Sept 2022](https://microstructure.exchange/papers/Schwartz_et_al_,_2022_WP,_The_'Actual_Retail_Price'_of_Equity_Trades.pdf)) **(a)**, fetched and parsed.
  - Their Q1 2022 Rule 606 table shows **IBKR and Fidelity with no PFOF for equity market orders**, "even though they do receive payments for other equity trades and for options". **Options account for the bulk of PFOF revenue.** IBKR Pro routes to various market centres plus IBKR's own ATS. IBKR Lite orders "are generally sent to 'OTC market makers', with the attendant PFOF" (same paper, pp. 13–14) **(a)**.
  - Robinhood earned about 72% of revenue from PFOF in that period; TD Ameritrade and E*Trade 15–20% (same paper) **(a)**.
- **How wholesalers win flow:** brokers choose a wholesaler "by a formula that depends on past execution quality... relationship... and other factors", not by a real-time price, and wholesalers do not display their prices. Much price improvement comes from sub-penny pricing that exchanges are not allowed to quote. **(a)** [SEC 34-96495 pp. 8–9, fn 8](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf).
- **Wholesalers' inventory risk:** they "expose themselves to inventory risk when internalizing order flow, but mitigate this risk by internalizing orders that possess low adverse selection risks". **(a)** [SEC 34-96495 p. 213](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf).
- **Scale economies:** "the largest wholesalers offer the lowest costs due to economies of scale". Liquidity-cost differences correlate with proxies for inventory cost. **(a)** [Dyhrberg, Shkilko & Werner, "The Retail Execution Quality Landscape" (JFE 2025; WP Dec 2022)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecl:ohidic:2022-14), abstract fetched.

### Inferences
- **(c) The "alpha in the quote" is a forecast of the next few minutes, applied per order.** A wholesaler can only price-improve and pay PFOF profitably if it can predict which orders carry information, and which way prices will drift in the seconds to minutes it holds inventory. The SEC's 1.26 vs 4.40 bps price-impact gap is the measured value of that sorting.
- **(c) Where the edge comes from, in order:**
  1. Privileged access to uninformed flow, through routing contracts with brokers.
  2. Netting buys against sells across millions of orders, so most inventory offsets internally and only the remainder is hedged on exchanges.
  3. Short-horizon prediction, latency and cross-asset hedging (options against stock, ETFs against baskets).
  4. Scale economies, per Dyhrberg et al.
  None of the four is available to a small retail trader.
- **(c) The retail trader's position.** A retail trader is the counterparty whose low toxicity is the product being sold. The way to keep more of that value is to trade where your order is treated as uninformed, cheaply (price improvement, midpoint), or to supply liquidity yourself with limit orders and auctions instead of paying the spread.

- I could not open Menkveld's full text. The per-trade euro profit split (spread earned vs positioning loss) that is often quoted from it is unverified here.
- I did not obtain Virtu's S-1 "losing days" statistic (folklore-adjacent; unverified) or Baron, Brogaard, Hagströmer & Kirilenko on HFT returns by latency.

## Quantitative picture of market-making P&L

### Takeaway
The public numbers describe a business of tiny margins per unit of volume. Wholesaler realised half-spreads are about 0.4–1.4 bps of notional on retail marketable orders, set against PFOF of about $0.001–0.003 per share. In 2024 the firm turned US$9.7bn of revenue into about US$5.2bn EBITDA on about US$16bn of trading capital. Per-share economics and inventory holding periods are not publicly disclosed.

### Cited Findings
- **Margin and capital:**
  - 2024: EBITDA US$5.2bn on US$9.7bn revenue, about a 54% EBITDA margin **(c)** arithmetic. Net income US$4.2bn, net trading capital US$16bn, total assets US$80.4bn **(b)** [Hedgeweek/Bloomberg](https://www.hedgeweek.com/griffins-citadel-securities-reports-record-9-7bn-trading-revenue/).
  - 2025: EBITDA US$6.5bn on US$12.2bn, about 53% **(c)**. Trading capital US$21bn **(b)** [Benzinga/Bloomberg](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/03/51466069/citadel-securities-sees-record-breaking-12-billion-in-trading-revenue-in-2025).
- **Per-trade economics for retail marketable orders:**
  - Wholesaler realised half-spread is +0.61 bps overall, +0.39 bps on market orders and +1.43 bps on marketable limits. Price impact is 1.20 bps (Q1 2022) **(a)** [SEC Table 5](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf).
  - Exchanges show a negative realised half-spread (−0.38 bps). The SEC notes this "does not necessarily mean" exchange market makers lose money, because rebates are excluded (fn 432) **(a)**.
- **Price improvement vs PFOF scale:** across brokers, price improvement varied from about **$0.03 to $0.08 per share**, an order of magnitude more than PFOF at **$0.001–$0.003 per share**. Variation in PFOF "cannot explain" variation in execution prices **(a)** [Schwarz et al.](https://microstructure.exchange/papers/Schwartz_et_al_,_2022_WP,_The_'Actual_Retail_Price'_of_Equity_Trades.pdf) (published as [J. Finance 80(5), 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13467)).
- **Aggregate size:** 1 bp of retail execution cost ≈ **US$2bn a year** in aggregate (Schwarz et al.) **(a)**. Wholesaler intermediation saves retail "close to a billion dollars per month" net of everything (Dyhrberg et al.) **(a)**. The SEC's competing estimate is a **US$1.5bn a year** shortfall versus a hypothetical auction regime **(a)**. These are three different counterfactuals and should not be added together.
- **Policy status:**
  - The SEC withdrew the Order Competition Rule and Regulation Best Execution proposals on **12 June 2025** **(a/b)** ([regulatoryandcompliance.com](https://www.regulatoryandcompliance.com/2025/06/sec-formally-withdraws-fourteen-rule-proposals/); [Stinson](https://www.stinson.com/newsroom-publications-sec-withdraws-proposed-rules-affecting-investment-advisers-funds-and-broker-dealers)).
  - The SEC did adopt (18 Sep 2024) a **$0.005 tick** for tick-constrained NMS stocks ≥ $1 (time-weighted average quoted spread ≤ 1.5¢) and cut the **access-fee cap from 30 mils to 10 mils** ($0.001/share) ([SEC fact sheet](https://www.sec.gov/files/34-101070-fact-sheet.pdf); [Final rule 34-101070](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2024/34-101070.pdf)) **(a/b)**.
  - The DC Circuit denied Cboe et al.'s challenge on 14 Oct 2025. The SEC then granted **temporary exemptive relief pushing both compliance dates to the first business day of November 2026** ([SEC Release 34-104172, 31 Oct 2025](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2025/34-104172.pdf), fetched and parsed) **(a)**. Atkins' statement of 15 Oct 2025 had flagged the review ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/atkins-101525-statement-regarding-minimum-pricing-increments-access-fee-caps)) **(a)**.

### Inferences
- **(c) Implied gross per share.** If wholesaler net margin on a typical retail market order is about 0.4 bps realised minus PFOF (0.002 ÷ ~$48 average price ≈ 0.4 bps), the 5-minute realised economics of PFOF-paying flow are close to zero. That makes the profit depend on what happens after 5 minutes (inventory netting, hedging, longer-horizon drift) and on non-PFOF flow. It explains why the firm's revenue scales with volatility and turnover.
- **(c) What the November 2026 change could do.** If it takes effect, half-penny ticks and lower access fees should narrow quoted spreads in the most liquid names and shrink the sub-penny price-improvement edge wholesalers have over exchanges. Both changes favour a retail trader who uses limit orders or routes to exchanges, as IBKR Pro does. The magnitude is unmeasured, and whether the date holds is unknown as of 13 Oct 2026.

### Gaps
- There is no public number for Citadel Securities' inventory half-life, gross vs net revenue per share, or loss-day frequency.
- The ~54% EBITDA margin is computed from Bloomberg-sourced, unaudited figures.

## Retail execution quality evidence, and what a retail trader can do

### Takeaway
Three findings stand out.
1. For small US market orders, wholesaler internalisation gives meaningful price improvement, about 24% of the quoted spread overall and 47% in S&P 500 names. Exchanges give about 3–5%.
2. Execution quality varies widely by broker for identical orders: a round trip costs 7–46 bps excluding commission.
3. In that experiment **IBKR Pro (SMART, no PFOF) was near the bottom on price for tiny market orders**, before its commissions.

So "no PFOF" does not automatically mean "better price" for small market orders. IBKR's advantages lie elsewhere: limit and midpoint orders, direct access to auctions, routing control and no conflict of interest. Those tools, not the absence of PFOF, are what cut costs for a monthly rebalancer.

### Cited Findings
- **Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion & Odean, "The 'Actual Retail Price' of Equity Trades"** (WP 13 Sep 2022; [J. Finance 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13467)). **(a)** [WP PDF](https://microstructure.exchange/papers/Schwartz_et_al_,_2022_WP,_The_'Actual_Retail_Price'_of_Equity_Trades.pdf), fetched and parsed.
  - **Design:** 85,000 simultaneous market orders across six accounts, Dec 2021–mid 2022, $100 and $1,000 orders. The median trade was 5 shares in a ~$20 stock with a $0.05 (~25 bp) spread, stratified across CRSP stocks.
  - **Mean round-trip cost excluding commission:** TD Ameritrade −0.072%; E*Trade −0.197%; Fidelity −0.234%; Robinhood −0.314%; **IBKR Pro −0.444%; IBKR Lite −0.462%**. The worst legal outcome, buying at the ask and selling at the bid, would be −0.619%.
  - **Price improvement:** IBKR Pro received it on 76% of trades but averaged only 18.8% of the spread, so a round trip cost about 62% of the quoted spread. TD averaged 47.2%, a round trip costing 5.6% of the spread.
  - **IBKR Pro commissions** averaged $0.35 per trade, about 35 bp of notional on the median 5-share trade.
  - **Cause:** dispersion arises because wholesalers give different prices to different brokers for the same trades. It is not explained by venue choice or PFOF.
- **Dyhrberg, Shkilko & Werner, "The Retail Execution Quality Landscape"** (JFE 2025; [SSRN 4313095](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4313095); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25000595)).
  - Wholesaler price improvement is **24% of the quoted spread** in the full sample (each side saves 12%, about **1.7¢/share**) and **47% in an average S&P 500 stock**, versus **3% / 5%** on exchanges. Exchanges have lower realised spreads, but price improvement dominates. **(a/b)** The headline is in the [fetched abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecl:ohidic:2022-14); the detailed percentages are from the search snippet.
- **SEC Table 5:** marketable orders routed to wholesalers have "higher fill rates, lower effective spreads, and lower E/Q ratios" **(a)** [34-96495](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/proposed/2022/34-96495.pdf). In 2022, the largest wholesalers' Rule 605 reports implied about US$3bn of retail savings ([SEC comment letter](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-32-22/s73222-221579-466122.pdf), snippet) **(b)**. The commenter is likely industry-aligned.
- **IBKR Lite vs Pro:** IBKR Lite is commission-free and routes to market makers that pay PFOF. IBKR Pro takes no PFOF and routes via SMART, charging per share or per trade. **(b)** Broker-review sites ([BrokerChooser](https://brokerchooser.com/education/investing/interactive-brokers-order-execution); [StockBrokers.com](https://www.stockbrokers.com/guides/order-execution)). This matches Schwarz et al.'s account description **(a)**, where IBKR Pro "charges commissions and has no PFOF" and IBKR Lite is "commission-free and use[s] PFOF".
- **The open is the most expensive time to trade.** Over 2010–2018, as volume migrated to the close, **turnover in the first 15 minutes fell about 22% for S&P 500 stocks**. **Effective spreads at the open widened about 10 bps** and **depth at the best quotes fell about 63%**. The open's share of price discovery (weighted price contribution) rose about 20%, which means more adverse selection at the open. **(a)** [Bogousslavsky & Muravyev WP June 2021, pp. 5, 24–25](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6310c0b9bb63a25599f4418c/t/634ffc92f81e226b2c30654f/1666186387645/who-trades-at-the-close_June2021.pdf), fetched and parsed. The same paper notes that spreads no longer follow a U-shape and are lower at the close (citing Upson & Van Ness 2017) **(a)**.
- **Tick-size evidence:** closing-auction price deviations rose sharply for small stocks after the 2016 Tick Size Pilot widened their tick, and the tick binds in 41.8% of auctions **(a)** (same paper, p. 4).

### Inferences
- **(c) For this project's US sleeve (monthly, IBKR, market orders):**
  - Marketable orders sent via IBKR Pro SMART go to lit venues. They tend to pay close to the full half-spread, with little price improvement, plus IBKR's per-share commission and pass-through exchange fees.
  - The cheaper paths, roughly in order:
    1. A **market-on-close (MOC) or limit-on-close (LOC)** order in the closing auction (next section).
    2. A **midpoint-pegged or passive limit order** placed during the day, away from the first 15–30 minutes.
    3. If market orders are kept, avoid the open entirely.
  - Switching to IBKR Lite for PFOF price improvement is **not** clearly better on this evidence: IBKR Lite was the worst account in Schwarz et al. Lite is also US-only, while ASX, LSE and TSX orders route the same way regardless.
- **(c) Size matters.** Schwarz et al.'s orders were tiny ($100–$1,000) and skewed to small stocks with ~25 bp spreads. A momentum book trading large caps in $1k–$20k clips faces large-cap spreads of about 1–3 bp half-spread (B&M: large-stock auction half-spread 1.47 bps). At those spreads, commission minimums and the fixed per-order fee, not spread, may dominate. This project's own cost model already uses US$1 floors and 5 bps of US slippage per side (`trading_algo/regions.py:96-98`). Against the measured large-cap half-spreads (~1.5 bps at the close), 5 bps per side looks conservative for liquid US names when executed in the auction. That needs checking against the actual universe's spreads before changing anything (see the project's "evidence over assertion" rule).
- **(c) The cheapest effective spread retail traders get comes from wholesalers, but only on small market orders.** As order size grows past the "covered order" range, wholesaler price improvement shrinks. The Rule 605 data cover orders under 10,000 shares only.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain Battalio & Jennings on wholesaler execution, or Battalio, Corwin & Jennings (2016) on make-take fees and broker routing. The latter is cited in Schwarz et al. as finding that limit orders sent to high-rebate venues execute worse.
- There is no direct evidence on IBKR Pro's execution of **limit, midpoint or MOC** orders. Schwarz et al. tested market orders only.
- IBKR's own Rule 605/606 statistics were not retrieved.
- I found no evidence on non-US (ASX, LSE, TSX) retail execution quality by broker. PFOF is banned in the UK (FCA) and is being banned in the EU (MiFIR review), but neither ban was sourced in this session.

## Closing-auction participation as a cost-reduction technique (US, UK, ASX, TSX)

### Takeaway
In the US the closing auction fills even large volumes at about the pre-close half-spread, with almost no extra price impact: on average 8.1 bps deviation versus a 7.6 bps half-spread, and only about 1.5 bps half-spread plus 1.2 bps impact in large caps. The deviation mostly reverses overnight. Auctions are a larger share of volume outside the US: about 35% of daily volume in developed Europe, roughly 30–40% of lit continuous volume on the LSE, and about 21–25% on the ASX. For a daily or monthly rebalancer that does not need intraday timing, the MOC/LOC auction is the cheapest transparent venue available to a retail account. Its costs are a fill price you do not know in advance and occasional large deviations on index-rebalance days.

### Cited Findings
- **Bogousslavsky & Muravyev, "Who Trades at the Close?"** (WP June 2021; published J. Financial Markets 2023, [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418123000502)). **(a)** [WP PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6310c0b9bb63a25599f4418c/t/634ffc92f81e226b2c30654f/1666186387645/who-trades-at-the-close_June2021.pdf), fetched and parsed.
  - **Volume:** the US closing auction was **7.5% of daily volume in 2018, up from 3.1% in 2010**, driven by ETFs and indexing.
  - **How close the auction price lands:** the closing price matches the pre-close best bid or ask in **68.5%** of auctions. Average absolute deviation from the 4pm midpoint is **8.1 bps** vs an average **half-spread of 7.56 bps**. Average **price impact is only 0.55 bps**. Large stocks show a 1.47 bps half-spread and 1.19 bps impact.
  - **By size:** average deviation ranges from **20.6 bps (small stocks) to 2.66 bps (large stocks)**. Deviations exceed the half-spread in 23.4% of cases and **exceed 63 bps in 1% of auctions**.
  - **Volume effect:** each +1% of auction turnover adds about 0.81 bps of deviation.
  - **Reversal:** closing deviations reverse almost fully overnight. About half the reversal comes within 30 minutes after the close for stocks with after-hours liquidity. In the top 1% of impact events (>19.7 bps), about 66% reverses overnight.
  - **Exchange design:** NYSE auction deviations are about 1.2 bps larger than Nasdaq's. The paper attributes this to imperfect competition: exchanges hold a "monopoly" on their auctions and charge higher fees, and NYSE floor brokers have exclusive D-orders.
  - **Index events:** on S&P 500 rebalancing days auction turnover is about 904% higher. Deviation rises by about 21 bps around index-change events, against a baseline of about 3 bps.
  - **Cost of rebalancing in the auction:** the published version's abstract reportedly says annualised trading costs for long/short anomaly portfolios are **17–41 bps, or 9–21 bps excluding microcaps**, when traded in closing auctions **(b)**. This comes from a search snippet of the published abstract and is not in the 2021 WP text I parsed.
- **Regional auction shares (Instinet data via State Street Investment Management):** closing share of daily volume at 25 Dec 2025 was **Developed Europe 34.82%, USA 15.39%, Asia ex-Japan 16.78%, Japan 23.8%**. In January 2018 it was Europe 19.59%, USA 8.40%, APAC 11.01%. SSGA: "Trading at the close is often cost-effective because spreads compress sharply in the final minutes". On rebalance days the close "can end up materially above or below" the last pre-auction trade. **(b)** [SSGA "Closing time"](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/institutional/insights/how-passive-investing-reshaping-microstructure), fetched. This is a vendor/industry note.
- **LSE:** closing-auction volume grew from about 30% to nearly 40% **of lit continuous volume** between Jan 2018 and Feb 2020. It did not rise in the March 2020 stress: "during periods of market stress, market participants do not necessarily turn to the closing auction". **(b)** [BMLL, 1 Jul 2021](https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/closing-auction-volumes-what-affects-closing-auction-volume-share), fetched; the denominator is lit continuous, not total volume. The LSE closing auction runs 16:30–16:35 ([LSE market close document](https://docs.londonstockexchange.com/sites/default/files/documents/market_close_information_document.pdf), snippet) **(b)**.
- **ASX:** the Closing Single Price Auction (CSPA) is about **21% of average daily turnover** (H1 2023), with 76–100 trades over A$1m on typical days ([ASX blog, 23 Aug 2023](https://www.asx.com.au/blog/finding-value-through-asx-closing-auctions), fetched) **(a)**. All ASX auctions were **25.3% of value traded** in the Dec 2024 quarter ([ASIC equity market data Q4 2024](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/markets/market-structure/equity-market-data/2024/equity-market-data-for-quarter-ending-december-2024/), snippet) **(b)**.
- **TSX:** runs a Market-on-Close facility, recently being modernised ([TSX MOC FAQ](https://www.tsx.com/en/resource/1/); [TSX MOC Modernization guide](https://www.tsx.com/en/resource/2357)) **(b)**. I found no share-of-volume figure.

### Inferences
- **(c) This fits the project's no-lookahead rule.** Trades already execute at t+1 (invariant #1). Submitting the t+1 orders as MOC/LOC in each sleeve's own closing auction keeps that rule and replaces "pay the spread at an uncertain intraday time" with about a half-spread at a published price. The fill price also equals the close the backtest marks to, so tracking error between backtest and paper fills would shrink.
- **(c) Size of the saving.** B&M's large-stock numbers (~1.5 bps half-spread + ~1.2 bps impact) against the open's ~10 bps wider effective spread suggest switching from opening market orders to MOC saves on the order of several bps per side in liquid names. It saves more in small and mid caps. For ASX/FTSE small caps the auction deviation can be 20 bps or more, but continuous trading in those names is worse.
- **(c) Risks:**
  - **Index-rebalance days:** avoid them, or use LOC limits, because deviations jump about 21 bps around S&P changes.
  - **Execution uncertainty:** an LOC can go unfilled.
  - **Overnight reversal:** if the strategy buys at the close on a high-imbalance day, part of the price paid reverses by morning. That is a real cost that a close-to-close backtest will not see.
  - **UK stamp duty (0.5%)** dominates all of this for the FTSE sleeve. Choosing between auction and continuous execution moves basis points against a fixed 50 bps duty on buys.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed cost study specific to the LSE, ASX or TSX closing auctions. The LSE and ASX shares come from exchange or vendor notes, and there is no TSX volume share.
- IBKR's MOC/LOC order cut-off times and fees per exchange were not retrieved.
- The 17–41 / 9–21 bps auction-rebalancing cost figure needs confirming in the published paper.

## Market making in FX and crypto: dealer spreads, last look, rollover, maker/taker fees

### Takeaway
In FX the "market maker's edge" also includes **last look**. Liquidity providers may reject a trade request after seeing it. The FX Global Code limits this to risk control, but a liquidity consumer still bears the cost of rejected trades. Spreads widen around the **5pm New York daily rollover**. That is **21:00 UTC during US daylight time and 22:00 UTC in winter**, when bank liquidity providers step back. In crypto the relevant structure is the exchange's maker/taker schedule. At retail tiers there are no maker rebates: on Binance maker = taker = 0.10% (0.075% with BNB), and on Kraken Pro's entry tier the taker fee is 0.80% vs 0.40% maker. Posting limit orders is the one piece of market-maker economics a small book can capture.

### Cited Findings
- **Last look, FX Global Code Principle 17:**
  - Last look is "a practice... whereby a Market Participant receiving a trade request has a final opportunity to accept or reject the request against its quoted price".
  - Price checks may be **symmetric or asymmetric**. Under an asymmetric check, an LP rejects if the price moved against it beyond tolerance but may fill at the old price if it moved in its favour.
  - The GFXC expects LPs to decide "without delay", and lists low acceptance rates, variable accept/reject times, and market movement against the liquidity consumer after a rejection as concerns.
  - A rejection "may have direct bearing on the execution cost" for the consumer.
  - **(a)** [GFXC Execution Principles WG Report on Last Look, Aug 2021](https://www.globalfxc.org/uploads/gfxc_report_last_look-1.pdf), fetched and parsed. Principle 17 restricts last look to risk control, "not for the purposes of managing profitability", and requires disclosure of typical hold times **(b)**, via search summaries of the Code and [BNY last look disclosure](https://www.bnymellon.com/content/dam/bnymellon/documents/pdf/disclaimers/global_fx_last_look_disclosure.pdf).
  - A large non-bank LP argues last look lets it quote tighter spreads ([XTX "Last Look Myths Debunked", May 2021](https://www.treasurers.org/system/files/XTX~LL%20paper~May%202021~Final~pblc%20.pdf)) **(b)**. This is self-interested and was not read in full.
- **Rollover spread widening:** spreads widen around 5pm New York as banks roll positions to the next value date and refresh systems, while Tier-1 LPs withdraw liquidity. Broker sources cite GBP/USD going from about 0.3 to 10 pips and crosses such as EUR/AUD widening 20–45 pips. **(b/c)** These are retail-broker blogs and forum posts only, so the figures are folklore-grade ([FP Markets](https://www.fpmarkets.com/blog/understanding-fx-spreads/); [Myfxbook forum](https://www.myfxbook.com/community/general/hour-every-day-your-spread/3366370,1); [FXNX](https://fxnx.com/en/blog/rollover-window-when-swap-posts-why-spreads-widen)). I found no BIS or academic measurement in this session.
- **Crypto exchange fees (primary pages fetched 13 Oct 2026):**
  - **Binance spot, regular user (VIP 0):** **0.100% maker / 0.100% taker**, or **0.075% / 0.075%** with the BNB 25% discount ([Binance fee schedule](https://www.binance.com/en/fee/schedule)) **(a)**.
  - **Kraken Pro spot:** **0.40% maker / 0.80% taker** at the $0+ 30-day-volume tier; 0.30/0.60 at $2.5k+; 0.22/0.38 at $10k+. Kraken's instant/simple interface charges 1% plus a spread ([Kraken fee schedule](https://www.kraken.com/features/fee-schedule)) **(a)**.
  - **Coinbase Advanced:** aggregators quote 0.50% maker / 0.90% taker at the entry tier, "since 16 Sep 2026" ([search summary of aggregators](https://www.kraken.com/learn/kraken-vs-coinbase)) **(b)**. Unverified, because the Coinbase help page returned 403. Another aggregator claimed "Binance 0% maker / 0.02% taker", which contradicts Binance's own page. That figure most likely refers to Binance.US or a promotion, so it is rejected here.
- **This project's crypto routing:** the default ccxt exchange for crypto execution and data is **Binance** (`trading_algo/forex/crypto_exec.py:174` `exchange: str = "binance"`; `crypto_exec.py:221`; `crypto_data.py:40`) **(a)**, read from the repo.
- **This project's FX session edge:** `trading_algo/forex/sessions.py:55` sets `_FX_EDGE_HOUR = 22`, with the comment "The FX week: opens ~22:00 UTC Sunday, closes ~22:00 UTC Friday" (line 54) **(a)**, read from the repo. The equity window comment at line 48–49 says it "is not DST-adjusted".

### Inferences
- **(c) The rollover hour moves with US daylight saving.** The FX day rolls at 17:00 New York time. That is 22:00 UTC only while New York is on EST (roughly Nov–Mar) and **21:00 UTC during EDT (roughly Mar–Nov)**. A fixed 22:00 UTC edge is an hour late for about eight months of the year. Any "avoid the rollover" rule for the FX book should be keyed to America/New_York 17:00, not a fixed UTC hour. This is a timezone fact. I have not measured the size of the spread spike.
- **(c) Small FX book.** A retail FX book through IBKR (IDEALPRO) or a retail broker gets aggregated dealer quotes, often with last look upstream. The practical defences are:
  1. Trade during the London/New York overlap, not at rollover or around the Sunday open.
  2. Use limit orders.
  3. Track the reject/re-quote rate as a cost.
- **(c) Small crypto book on Binance.** A round trip costs **20 bps in taker fees (15 bps with BNB) plus half-spreads**. Maker orders are charged the same 0.10%, so posting liquidity saves only the spread, not fees. That is still worth a few bps on majors and more on alts. On Kraken's entry tier the maker/taker gap is 40 bps per side, so post-only limit orders would roughly halve fee cost there. No venue fetched here offers retail-tier maker **rebates**. Rebates belong to institutional market-maker programmes, the crypto equivalent of Citadel's exchange-rebate and PFOF economics, and a small book cannot reach them.
- **(c) Pulling it together for the gap analysis:**
  - **Reachable:**
    - Being uninformed flow that gets price-improved (small US market orders at a good PFOF broker).
    - Supplying liquidity instead of taking it: limit and midpoint orders, maker orders in crypto.
    - Concentrating trades where liquidity pools: closing auctions in all four equity regions, the London/NY overlap in FX.
    - Avoiding the expensive windows: the open, the 5pm NY rollover, index-rebalance closes.
  - **Not reachable:**
    - Privileged order-flow contracts.
    - Internal netting at scale.
    - Sub-millisecond prediction and hedging.
    - Exchange-rebate tiers and DMM/LMM status.
    - Last-look optionality.
    - Funding at about SOFR + 200bp on US$4bn and US$16–21bn of trading capital.

### Gaps
- I found no academic measurement of FX spread widening at the 17:00 NY rollover, or of last-look hold-time and reject-rate distributions. A BIS FX execution report or a study such as Oomen (2017) on last look should be checked.
- Coinbase Advanced's current fee schedule is unverified.
- Binance maker/taker for the specific symbols the book trades (some pairs carry zero-fee promotions) was not checked.
- Funding-rate costs on perpetual futures, if the book uses them, are out of scope here.
