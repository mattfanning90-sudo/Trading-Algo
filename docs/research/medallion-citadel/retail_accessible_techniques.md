# Retail-accessible "institutional" techniques for a 12-1 cross-sectional momentum book

Scope: published, citable evidence on improvements to a monthly 12-1 momentum strategy (top ~10-12 names, inverse-vol weights, vol target, binary index-trend regime filter, drawdown breaker; US / UK / ASX / TSX sleeves; mostly long-only; AUD reporting). Research date: 2026-10-13.

Read-me-first caveat for the report writer: almost every headline number below comes from **long-short, value-weighted factor portfolios** (buy the top 30% or top decile, short the bottom), usually **gross of trading costs** and built from **hundreds to thousands of stocks**. None of the studies tests a 10-12-stock long-only book. Each finding therefore carries a transfer risk to this system; the per-section Inferences spell out which parts transfer and which do not.

## 1. Residual / idiosyncratic momentum

### Takeaway
Ranking on returns after removing factor exposure ("residual" or "idiosyncratic" momentum) is the best-documented, cheapest-to-implement enhancement: in a uniform 48-country test it roughly doubles the long-short Sharpe ratio, cuts maximum drawdown most of any method, and does so in the UK, Australia and Canada as well as the US. All of that evidence is long-short and gross of costs, and the extra turnover is real.

### Cited Findings
- Original paper: Blitz, Huij & Martens, "Residual Momentum", *Journal of Empirical Finance* 18(3), 2011, pp. 506-521. It reports that conventional momentum has large time-varying exposures to the Fama-French factors and that "roughly half of the risk of a conventional momentum strategy comes from these time-varying factor exposures." — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:empfin:v:18:y:2011:i:3:p:506-521)
- Secondary summary of the 2011 result: "idiosyncratic momentum exhibits only half of the volatility of standard momentum without any significant decrease in returns" (Hanauer & Windmüller describing Blitz et al. 2011). — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, p.4](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Construction used across Gutierrez & Pirinsky (2007), Blitz et al. (2011) and Blitz et al. (2018/2020): regress each stock's last 36 months of returns on the country's Fama-French three factors (market, size, value); sum the residuals from month t-12 to t-2 and divide by their standard deviation; rank on that. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, eqs. 8-9](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Gutierrez & Pirinsky (2007) found firm-specific (idiosyncratic) momentum "experiences no long-term reversals" (as summarised by Hanauer & Windmüller; I did not open the original). — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Update: Blitz, Hanauer & Vidojevic, "The idiosyncratic momentum anomaly", *International Review of Economics and Finance* 69 (2020), pp. 932-957: idiosyncratic momentum is priced after controlling for established and recent factors, including those that explain momentum-related anomalies; it is "a global phenomenon", including in Japan where conventional momentum fails. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:reveco:v:69:y:2020:i:c:p:932-957); [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/the-idiosyncratic-momentum-anomaly/)
- Practitioner summary of the same work: idiosyncratic momentum "produces only slightly lower returns but greatly reduces crash risk and results in an almost doubling of the Sharpe ratio." (Search-snippet summary of Alpha Architect / Swedroe; the page itself returned 403 so I could not read it in full.) — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/swedroe-spotlight-enhancing-momentum-strategies-via-idiosyncratic-momentum/)
- Robeco (where Blitz and Hanauer work) publishes the idiosyncratic-momentum factor data set. Note the authors' affiliation: a disclosed conflict of interest. — [Robeco data set](https://www.robeco.com/za/insights/2020/06/data-sets-the-idiosyncratic-momentum-factor.html); disclosure in [Hanauer & Windmüller draft, p.1](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- **Uniform head-to-head test** (Hanauer & Windmüller, "Enhanced Momentum Strategies"; 2019 working-paper draft read here, later published in *Journal of Banking & Finance* vol. 148, 2023, so published figures may differ). Long-short, value-weighted 2x3 size/momentum sorts, gross of costs. US 1930-2017, Table 2 Panel A: conventional MOM Sharpe 0.47, max drawdown -67.10%, skew -1.91; iMOM (idiosyncratic) Sharpe 0.95, max drawdown -25.52%, skew 0.14. Global (48 countries) 1991-2017, Panel B: MOM Sharpe 0.57 vs iMOM 1.65. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, Table 2](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf); [published version record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v148y2023ics0378426622002928.html)
- Same paper: "For the broad sample, we document that idiosyncratic momentum outperforms all other strategies, as the improvements in Sharpe ratio and t-statistic for idiosyncratic momentum are more than twice that of the improvements of volatility-scaling strategies and the reduction in maximum drawdowns is the highest." — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, p.20](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Country-level annualised Sharpe ratios, 1991-2017, long-short, gross (Table 11 Panel A; columns MOM / constant-vol-scaled 6M / dynamic / iMOM): **Australia 1.28 / 1.51 / 1.44 / 1.78; UK 0.79 / 1.26 / 1.18 / 1.32; Canada 0.71 / 1.10 / 1.01 / 1.10**; US (1930-2017) 0.47 / 0.86 / 0.88 / 0.95. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, Table 11](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Turnover and costs (Table 6): iMOM turnover 65.32%/month vs MOM 53.79% (US); 62.59% vs 50.32% (global). Break-even round-trip cost at the 5% significance level: US MOM 0.62% vs iMOM 0.77%; global MOM 0.35% vs iMOM 0.87%. The authors state this "does not explicitly test the after-trading cost performance". — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, Table 6 and §3.3](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Scaling iMOM by its own realised volatility "further increases its Sharpe ratio"; the vol-scaling alpha is distinct from the iMOM alpha. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, p.7](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- A replication titled "Residual Momentum and Reversal Strategies Revisited" (Huij & Lansdorp, SSRN 2929306) exists; I could not read it (403). — [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=2929306)
- A 2024 paper revisits residual momentum in Japan (*Finance Research Letters* vol. 93 per RePEc); title only, not read. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v93y2024ics1057521924001224.html)
- Novy-Marx & Velikov's cost table lists an "Idiosyncratic Momentum" strategy among the high-frequency anomalies; I did not extract its net numbers. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)

### Inferences
- **How much is out-of-sample.** The international 1991-2017 sample and the post-2011 US years are out-of-sample relative to the 2011 paper, and the effect held in both. That is real post-publication evidence, but from the same research group (Robeco-affiliated). I found no independent replication I could read.
- **Data needs for retail.** The method needs only each stock's monthly returns plus factor returns. A market-only version (regress on the local index, as this system already loads the regime index) needs no extra data. The full three-factor version needs local size and value factor returns. Ken French's data library publishes regional (not single-country) developed-market factors; that is from my own knowledge and was not checked in this session.
- **Transfer to a 10-12-stock long-only book.** The Sharpe-ratio doubling is measured on a long-short spread where the factor exposures cancel. In a long-only top-12 book the gain shows up differently: the ranking stops choosing stocks just because they have high beta in a rising market, so the book should carry less beta and less sector clustering at turning points. The size of that effect for a concentrated long-only book is not published (see Gaps).
- **Interaction with the existing inverse-vol weights.** Dividing by residual volatility is itself a risk adjustment. Inverse-vol weighting already exists in the system, so part of the gain may already be captured. The two are not identical: one changes *which* stocks are chosen, the other *how much* of each.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests residual momentum on a concentrated (10-20 name) long-only portfolio, or net of retail-scale costs.
- Could not read Huij & Lansdorp's replication (403), so whether residual momentum held up independently after 2011 is unverified here.
- Exact 2011 Sharpe ratios and sample period from Blitz, Huij & Martens were not confirmed from the paper itself (only via a secondary summary).
- The country Sharpe ratios come from a 2019 draft; I did not check the published 2023 version.

## 2. Volatility-managed / risk-managed momentum vs a binary regime filter

### Takeaway
Scaling momentum exposure inversely to its own recent volatility (Barroso & Santa-Clara) roughly doubled the Sharpe ratio in-sample and roughly halved worst drawdowns. Momentum is the one factor where this partly survives transaction costs. The broad claim that vol-management works for any factor did **not** survive real-time testing (Cederburg et al. 2020). Daniel & Moskowitz's "panic state" indicator (past two-year market return negative AND high volatility) is the academic cousin of a binary regime filter; the sizing based on it is continuous, not on/off.

### Cited Findings
- Barroso & Santa-Clara, "Momentum has its moments" (*JFE* 2015), US long-short momentum: Sharpe 0.53 unmanaged vs 0.97 risk-managed; excess kurtosis 18.24 → 2.68; skew -2.47 → -0.42; maximum drawdown -96.69% → -45.20%. Scaling uses momentum's own realised volatility over the previous six months of daily returns. — [Barroso & Santa-Clara PDF](http://www.snifferquant.com/gyantal/Incode/papers/Momentum%20Has%20Its%20Moments(scaling%20Momentum%20by%20vol),2014.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256017573_Momentum_Has_Its_Moments)
- Hanauer & Windmüller describe Barroso & Santa-Clara as finding "Sharpe ratios more than double, while portfolio turnover only marginally increases"; their own Table 6 shows US turnover rising from 53.79% (MOM) to 80.63% (constant-vol 6M) per month. That is not marginal in their construction. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, p.3 and Table 6](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Daniel & Moskowitz, "Momentum Crashes" (*JFE* 2016; NBER w20439), US 1927-2013: crashes "occur in 'panic' states – following market declines and when market volatility is high – and are contemporaneous with market rebounds." An "implementable dynamic momentum strategy based on forecasts of momentum's mean and variance approximately doubles the alpha and Sharpe Ratio of a static momentum strategy." Across all markets and asset classes the dynamic strategy's Sharpe ratio is 1.18, "four times larger than that of the static momentum strategy applied to US equities". Gross of costs. — [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- Daniel & Moskowitz define the bear-market indicator I_B = 1 if the cumulative past two-year market return is negative. Panic states interact that with market variance, and momentum's mean return "falls by 0.518 (t-stat = -28.4) in panic states". — [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- **In-sample caveat on Daniel & Moskowitz:** they "exploit ex-post information for calibrating their baseline model". Re-estimated out-of-sample by Hanauer & Windmüller, the dynamic Sharpe falls from 0.95 (in-sample) to 0.88 (US 1930-2017) and from 1.08 to 0.85 (global 1991-2017). Turnover is 82.22%/month vs 53.79% for plain momentum, and the strategy needs leverage and can go net short. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, fn. 5, 35 and Tables 2, 6](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Moreira & Muir, "Volatility-Managed Portfolios" (*JF* 2017): portfolios that take less risk when volatility is high produce large alphas for market, value, momentum, profitability, ROE, investment and FX carry. These are in-sample spanning-regression alphas. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2659431); [NBER w22208](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22208/w22208.pdf)
- **Critique:** Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan, "On the performance of volatility-managed portfolios" (*JFE* 2020), test 103 equity strategies and find "no statistical or economic evidence that volatility-managed portfolios systematically earn higher Sharpe ratios than their unmanaged counterparts" when the strategy is run in real time. The in-sample alphas survive spanning regressions, but the implied real-time strategies do not deliver. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X2030132X); [Univ. of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/on-the-performance-of-volatility-managed-portfolios/); [author PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)
- **Costs:** Barroso & Detzel, "Do limits to arbitrage explain the benefits of volatility-managed portfolios?" (*JFE* 140(3), 2021): after transaction costs, even with five cost-mitigation methods, volatility management of factors other than the market "generally produces zero abnormal returns". Only the market and **momentum** portfolios are "partially robust to transaction costs". — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3088828); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v140y2021i3p744-767.html)
- DeMiguel, Martin-Utrera & Uppal, "A Multifactor Perspective on Volatility-Managed Portfolios" (*JF* 79(6), 2024): a later rebuttal-style paper. I did not read it, so its findings are not reported here. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13395)
- Two further papers appear by title only (not read): "The disappearing profitability of volatility-managed equity factors" (*J. Financial Markets*) and "On the performance of volatility-managed equity factors — International and further evidence" (*J. Empirical Finance*, 2024). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418123000551); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092753982400094X)

### Inferences
- **What survives for this system:** momentum-specific risk scaling. Momentum is the factor where the effect is strongest, and it is the one Barroso & Detzel found partly robust to costs. Generic "vol-target everything" does not survive real-time testing (Cederburg et al.).
- **Binary filter vs continuous scaling.** A binary index-trend filter fires on the same condition Daniel & Moskowitz identify (the market has fallen). But their evidence says the danger is the *rebound out of* a bear market, when losers surge. A long-only book that is flat because the filter is off misses the rebound but is not hurt by the losers surging. A continuous scale-down (smaller, not zero) is the better-evidenced design. Nobody has published a head-to-head of "binary trend filter vs continuous vol scaling" on a long-only momentum book.
- The system already vol-targets the *portfolio*. Barroso & Santa-Clara scale by momentum's *own* realised volatility (the long-short spread). For a long-only book the closest analogue is the realised volatility of the book itself, which is mostly market volatility. That makes it a market-timing overlay more than a momentum-crash overlay, and that difference matters for how much of the published gain transfers.

### Gaps
- No published test of vol scaling on a concentrated long-only momentum book.
- No published head-to-head of a binary 200-day/index-trend regime filter vs Barroso-Santa-Clara scaling.
- Barroso & Santa-Clara's exact sample period was not re-verified from the paper in this session (commonly cited as 1927-2011).

## 3. Beta hedging and long-only vs long-short: how much of long-only momentum is just market beta?

### Takeaway
About half of long-short momentum profit comes from the long (winner) side, and that half is stable across firm size and across 86 years, so long-only momentum is a real premium, not just leverage. But a long-only winners book still carries roughly full market beta. In the US data, the winner decile's ~15%/yr excess return is about half market premium (~7.6%) and half momentum premium (~7.7%). The famous crashes happen mostly on the *short* side, so a long-only book feels them as large *relative* underperformance in rebounds, not as absolute collapses. Ex-ante beta hedging does not prevent crashes.

### Cited Findings
- Israel & Moskowitz, "The role of shorting, firm size, and time on market anomalies" (*JFE* 108(2), 2013): "long positions make up almost all of size, 60% of value, and half of momentum profits". Shorting becomes *less* important for momentum as firm size decreases. Momentum profits "exhibit no reliable relation with size". Robust over 86 years of US data and ~40 years across four international equity markets and five asset classes. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X12002401); [EconPapers](http://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:108:y:2013:i:2:p:275-301)
- Momentum was persistent in all four 20-year sub-periods, including the two decades after the original momentum studies were published. — [Israel & Moskowitz via search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256022216_The_Role_of_Shorting_Firm_Size_and_Time_on_Market_Anomalies)
- Asness, Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz, "Fact, Fiction and Momentum Investing" (*JPM* 40(5), 2014) reject the myth that "momentum can only be exploited on the short side": the long and short sides are about equally profitable over 1927-2013 US data and 40 years of international data. — [AQR PDF](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/JPM-Fact-Fiction-and-Momentum-Investing.pdf?sc_lang=en); [SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=2435323)
- Daniel & Moskowitz, US 1927-2013 (value-weighted deciles, gross): winner-decile excess return averages 15.3%/yr, loser decile -2.5%/yr, market 7.6%/yr; WML (winners-minus-losers) Sharpe 0.71 vs market 0.40; WML beta -0.58 unconditionally. — [NBER w20439, p.7](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- Crashes come from the short leg: "the crash performance is mostly attributable to the short side or the performance of losers… a crash where the short side of the portfolio – the losers – are 'crashing up' rather than down." In March-May 2009 the market rose 26%, losers rose 163% and winners only 8%. — [NBER w20439, pp.5-6](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- "The predominant source of this optionality comes from the loser portfolio". In bear markets, the loser decile's up-market beta is 2.160 vs a slightly *negative* up-market beta increment for the winner decile (-0.215). — [NBER w20439, Table 4](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- **Beta hedging:** Grundy & Martin (2001) showed hedging momentum's dynamic market and size exposures cuts volatility without losing return, but Daniel & Moskowitz show this "depends on using ex-post factor betas". Their ex-ante hedged portfolio "is not only unable to avoid or mitigate the crash" (Figure 4). — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, pp.2-3](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf); [NBER w20439, §3.4](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)

### Inferences
- Roughly half of a long-only winner book's gross excess return is the equity premium the investor would get from an index fund (15.3% vs 7.6%, US 1927-2013, gross). This is arithmetic on the Daniel & Moskowitz table, not a beta-adjusted alpha. The winner decile's beta is not 1, so the true alpha split is not exactly 50/50.
- For this system, "beta hedging" (shorting the index against a long-only book) would remove the equity premium and keep only the winners-minus-market spread. Published evidence says the ex-ante version does not stop crashes, and for a long-only book the crash leg (losers) is not held anyway. Residual momentum (section 1) is the better-evidenced way to reduce *unintended* beta, because it changes stock selection rather than adding a hedge.
- A retail long-short or market-neutral momentum book takes on the side that carries the crash risk (shorting losers), plus borrow costs and short-sale constraints. The evidence that shorting matters *less* for momentum than for value (Israel & Moskowitz) argues that long-only is the right default at retail scale.

### Gaps
- No published estimate of the CAPM beta of a top-10-12 long-only momentum basket specifically.
- No per-country split of long-side vs short-side momentum profit for UK/Australia/Canada was found (Israel & Moskowitz's four international markets were not individually verified here).

## 4. Signal combination: momentum + value / quality / low-vol / reversal

### Takeaway
Value and momentum are strongly negatively correlated (about -0.6), and a 50/50 combination beats either alone in every market Asness-Moskowitz-Pedersen tested. Combining also *lowers* turnover per unit of return. For long-only books, AQR's evidence says to build one combined score per stock ("integrate"), not to hold separate single-factor sleeves side by side ("mix"). Value turned out to be a poor hedge in the November 2020 momentum crash.

### Cited Findings
- Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, "Value and Momentum Everywhere" (*JF* 2013): consistent value and momentum premia across eight markets/asset classes. The value-momentum correlation is "strongly negative, averaging about –0.60", and "in every market, the value/momentum combination outperforms either value or momentum by itself." — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Value-and-Momentum-Everywhere); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.12021); [author PDF](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)
- Novy-Marx & Velikov (*RFS* 2016), US 7/1963-12/2013, value-weighted long-short deciles: plain momentum gross 1.33%/mo, turnover 34.5%/mo (avg per side), cost 0.65%/mo, **net 0.68%/mo**. A value+momentum+profitability combo ("ValMomProf"): gross 1.43%/mo, turnover 26.8%, cost 0.43%, **net 0.99%/mo**. Value+momentum ("ValMom"): gross 0.93%, turnover 28.7%, net 0.51%/mo. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, Table 3 Panel B](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)
- Fitzgibbons, Friedman, Pomorski & Serban, "Long-Only Style Investing: Don't Just Mix, Integrate" (*Journal of Investing* 26(4), 2017): the integrated portfolio (aggregate style ranks into one score per stock, then build the portfolio) "substantially improves returns and information ratio" vs a mix of separate single-style portfolios, "by avoiding stocks with offsetting style exposures". AQR authors, AQR-published. — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Long-Only-Style-Investing); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2802849)
- Daniel & Moskowitz: HML (value) has opposite-signed market exposure in panic states, but "the combination of HML and WML does not completely hedge the panic-state optionality". — [NBER w20439, factor-exposure regressions in panic states](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- November 2020: Risk.net headline "Vaccine rally showed value flawed as momentum hedge, quant says" (paywalled; headline only). — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/investing/quant-investing/7714536/vaccine-rally-showed-value-flawed-as-momentum-hedge-quant-says)
- Asness et al. (2014) conclude momentum "works best when combined with value" (search summary of "Fact, Fiction and Momentum Investing"). — [AQR PDF](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/JPM-Fact-Fiction-and-Momentum-Investing.pdf?sc_lang=en)
- Globe and Mail practitioner screen (Canada): a "Blended Momentum" portfolio gained an average 16.6%/yr from 2000 to October 2022. Newspaper back-test; cost treatment and survivorship handling unknown; not peer-reviewed. — [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/markets/inside-the-market/article-searching-for-stocks-with-momentum-but-reduced-risk/)

### Inferences
- Value and quality need **fundamental data** (book value, earnings, profitability) point-in-time for four regions. That is the one real data gap vs institutions for this technique; price-only signals (low volatility, short-term reversal) need nothing new.
- Combining is the only enhancement in these notes that improves return *and* lowers turnover in the Novy-Marx & Velikov numbers (ValMomProf turnover 26.8% vs momentum 34.5%; net 0.99% vs 0.68%/mo). That is US long-short data; for a 12-name book the integrated score would mostly act as a tie-breaker among strong-momentum names.
- Value as a crash hedge has a mixed record. Daniel & Moskowitz say it is incomplete; the 2020 vaccine rally is reported to have exposed it. Don't present it as insurance.

### Gaps
- No search on momentum + low-volatility or momentum + short-term-reversal effect sizes specifically (e.g., combining with 1-month reversal); not covered.
- Which individual-stock universes the AMP 2013 paper uses (I believe US, UK, Continental Europe, Japan) was not re-verified this session, so UK-specific value+momentum numbers are not cited.
- No retail-scale (10-30 name) evidence on integrated multi-factor portfolios was found.

## 5. Rebalance timing luck and staggered (tranched) rebalancing

### Takeaway
Rebalance timing luck is the return difference between identical strategies rebalanced on different days. It is largest for momentum among the common factors, and it grows with concentration and turnover. Splitting the book into N staggered sub-portfolios ("tranches") cuts it by roughly 1/N. This is a noise-reduction technique, not a return enhancer: expected return is unchanged.

### Cited Findings
- Hoffstein, Sibears & Faber, "Rebalance Timing Luck: The Difference Between Hired and Fired" (SSRN, October 2018): defines timing luck as the standard deviation of returns across identically managed portfolios rebalanced on different dates. For a 60/40 portfolio (1922-June 2018) it is 0.36%/yr, and May rebalancing produced over 20% more terminal wealth than October rebalancing. Ex-ante expected returns of the sub-indexes are identical, and differences do not mean-revert. "N such portfolios suppress this noise by 1/N." — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3319045.); [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/calendar-effects/rebalance-timing-noise/)
- Hoffstein, Faber & Braun, "Rebalance Timing Luck: The Dumb (Timing) Luck of Smart Beta" (Epsilon Theory, 17 Aug 2020; US long-only factor portfolios, July 2000-Sept 2019): annualised timing luck for semi-annually rebalanced 100-stock portfolios was **momentum 4.4%**, value 2.5%, quality 2.0%, low-vol 1.1%. For replicated S&P factor indices (2001-2019) the spread was momentum 192 bp, quality 106 bp, value 100 bp, low-vol 25 bp. They report "a twenty percentage-point return difference" over a rolling 252-day window from shifting the rebalance by one month. Timing luck rises with turnover and concentration and falls with more frequent rebalancing. — [Panoptica/Epsilon Theory](https://www.panoptica.com/rebalance-timing-luck-the-dumb-timing-luck-of-smart-beta-by-hoffstein-faber-and-braun/?from_src=epsilontheory); [PDF copy](https://foro.masdividendos.com/uploads/short-url/tmXvnwshpt1jZS93nEyiJZuuAD7.pdf)
- Hoffstein: rebalance frequency "should really be derived as a function of signal decay speed and cost. e.g. a momentum portfolio likely has to rebalance more frequently than a value portfolio." — [X/Twitter](https://x.com/choffstein/status/1196126894732664832)
- A 2026 paper, "The Tranching Dilemma: A Cost-Aware Approach to Mitigate Rebalance [timing luck]" (Concretum Group), addresses the cost of tranching; not read. — [Concretum PDF](https://concretumgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/The-Tranching-Dilemma.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 10-12-stock momentum sleeve is far more concentrated than the 100-stock portfolios measured (4.4%/yr luck at semi-annual rebalance). Monthly rebalancing reduces luck, but concentration increases it, so the system's backtest Sharpe likely has a material "which day of the month" component. A cheap retail check is to re-run the backtest with the rebalance shifted by 1-20 trading days and look at the spread.
- Tranching at retail scale costs more: each tranche trades a quarter (for 4 tranches) of the capital, so the per-trade commission floor bites harder, and more names are held. For small sleeves the commission floor (invariant #4 in this repo) is the binding constraint. The Concretum paper's title suggests it tackles exactly this cost trade-off.
- Tranching needs no new data or execution capability, only more, smaller orders.

### Gaps
- No published timing-luck figure for a monthly-rebalanced, 10-20-name momentum portfolio, or for non-US markets.
- Could not read the Concretum "Tranching Dilemma" paper.

## 6. Turnover reduction and cost-aware rebalancing (buffers / no-trade bands)

### Takeaway
A buy/hold spread (enter only names in the top 10%, keep them until they fall out of the top 20%) is the single most effective cost-mitigation technique in Novy-Marx & Velikov. For US momentum it cut turnover from 34.5% to 18.8% a month and raised the net return from 0.68% to 0.85% a month. Gârleanu & Pedersen give the theory: trade partially toward a target that "aims in front of" where the signal is heading.

### Cited Findings
- Novy-Marx & Velikov, "A Taxonomy of Anomalies and Their Trading Costs" (*RFS* 2016; data 7/1963-12/2013): "Introducing a buy/hold spread, with more stringent requirements for establishing positions than for maintaining them, is the most effective cost mitigation technique." "Most anomalies with turnover less than 50% per month generate significant net spreads when designed to mitigate transaction costs; few with higher turnover do." — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, abstract](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf); [NBER w20721](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20721/w20721.pdf)
- Momentum, plain decile strategy: gross 1.33%/mo, turnover 34.5%, costs 0.65%, net 0.68%/mo. Momentum with a 10%/20% buy/hold spread: gross 1.20%/mo, turnover 18.8%, costs 0.35%, **net 0.85%/mo**. Value-weighted long-short; costs from effective-spread estimates. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, Tables 3 and 4](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)
- "as the buy/hold spread increases the past performance spread between the winners and losers portfolios narrows, but… the turnover and transaction cost reductions are even more dramatic, resulting in increasing net spreads and Sharpe ratios" (Appendix Fig. A1, momentum example). — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, App. C.1](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)
- Restricting to low-cost stocks is a second mitigation technique they test. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, App. C.2](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)
- Gârleanu & Pedersen, "Dynamic Trading with Predictable Returns and Transaction Costs" (*JF* Dec 2013): the optimal portfolio is a linear combination of the current portfolio, the no-cost optimum and the optimum based on future expected returns. Slower-decaying signals get more weight, and investors should "aim in front of the target". — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1364170); [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Dynamic-Trading-With-Predictable-Returns-and-Transactions-Costs)
- Chen & Velikov's 120-anomaly study applies the same buy/hold methodology (title only, not read). — [Chen, "Accounting for the Anomaly Zoo"](https://jacobslevycenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Accounting-for-the-Anomaly-Zoo.pdf)
- Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz, "Trading Costs of Asset Pricing Anomalies" (SSRN 2294498) uses live institutional execution data; Hanauer & Windmüller cite it as showing that standard momentum is profitable after real costs. Not read directly. — [SSRN](https://ssrn.com/abstract=2294498); [Hanauer & Windmüller draft, p.26](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)

### Inferences
- Of everything in these notes, the buy/hold buffer transfers most directly to retail: it needs no new data, it lowers the number of tickets (and so the per-trade commission floor costs), and it lengthens holding periods. That last point matters in this repo, where `tax.py` found median holds of 19-33 days and 0 of 50 round-trips reaching the 12-month CGT discount.
- The translation for a top-12 book: buy only names ranked in the top ~12 but keep a holding until it drops below, say, rank ~24. The thresholds should be set on the real panel, not copied from the paper's 10%/20% deciles.
- Gârleanu-Pedersen's partial-trading rule is the formal version of the same idea. A simple retail implementation is to trade a fraction of the gap to target each month, or to skip trades below a minimum weight change.

### Gaps
- Novy-Marx & Velikov costs are US effective spreads for institutional-sized trades. No equivalent buffer study was found for ASX/LSE/TSX, or with a retail fixed commission floor plus UK stamp duty.
- I did not extract their capacity numbers.

## 7. Calendar effects with surviving evidence

### Takeaway
The turn-of-the-month effect was enormous historically (1926-2005: all the US equity premium arrived in a four-day window; present in 31 of 35 countries) but appears to have **largely faded in US data since publication**, persisting more in international/emerging markets. Momentum's profits accrue **overnight**, not intraday (Lou, Polk & Skouras), which matters for *when* to place orders but is not a separate tradeable strategy at retail scale. No usable source was found on the pre-holiday effect.

### Cited Findings
- McConnell & Xu, "Equity Returns at the Turn of the Month" (*FAJ* 2008): in US equities 1926-2005, "investors received no reward for bearing market risk except at turns of the month" (four trading days: the last day of the month plus the first three of the next). It holds over 1897-2005 too; it is not confined to small or low-price stocks or to year/quarter ends; it occurs in 31 of 35 countries. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1135217); [author PDF](https://www.chesler.us/resources/academia/turn_of_the_month_stock_returns.pdf)
- Post-publication check (QuantSeeker practitioner blog, ETF data): for the standard [0:+3] window in recent data, "none of these differences are statistically significant". A wider [-3:+3] window shows equity ETFs with "approximately 5-12 basis points higher" average daily returns in the turn-of-month window; the effect "remains particularly pronounced in international and emerging markets" (tested markets include EWU/UK, EWA/Australia, EWC/Canada). Assumes 5 bp one-way costs. A blog, not peer-reviewed. — [QuantSeeker](https://www.quantseeker.com/p/turn-of-the-month-strategies-do-they)
- A 2026 paper, "Infrequent rebalancing, risk deferral, and equity returns at the turn of the month", offers a rebalancing-flow explanation (title only, not read). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1042443126000259)
- Lou, Polk & Skouras, "A tug of war: Overnight versus intraday expected returns" (*JFE* 134(1), 2019): "momentum profits accrue entirely overnight while profits on all other trading strategies studied occur entirely intraday". Average excess overnight momentum return 0.95%/mo (t = 2.95) vs intraday 0.04%/mo. Variation linked to institutional momentum trading generates ~2%/mo variation in overnight-minus-intraday momentum returns. — [NBER conference PDF](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2015/APf15/Lou_Polk_Skouras.pdf); [HKUST record](https://researchportal.hkust.edu.hk/en/publications/a-tug-of-war-overnight-versus-intraday-expected-returns/)

### Inferences
- For a monthly-rebalanced momentum book, Lou-Polk-Skouras implies fill timing has a small but signed effect. Buying new winners at the close (and selling exits at the open) keeps the overnight leg where momentum earns its return; buying at the next open gives up one night. Over a roughly one-month hold this is a single night, so the effect is small. It is an execution-detail refinement, not a strategy.
- The turn-of-month effect could inform *which day* to rebalance (connected to section 5's timing luck), but the post-2008 US evidence is weak and comes from a blog. It should not be presented as a surviving edge without a test on this system's own panel.

### Gaps
- No source found on the pre-holiday effect's post-publication survival or cost-adjusted profitability.
- No peer-reviewed post-2008 out-of-sample test of turn-of-month for UK/Australia/Canada was found.
- I did not find whether Lou-Polk-Skouras's overnight result has been replicated outside the US.

## 8. Momentum in Australia, the UK and Canada

### Takeaway
Long-short momentum is present and, on 1991-2017 data, **stronger in Australia, the UK and Canada than in the US** (Sharpe 1.28 / 0.79 / 0.71 vs 0.47 for the US over 1930-2017; gross of costs). Residual momentum raises each of them. The UK evidence shows momentum can be absent for decades (1955-76). Australian evidence is described as "contradictory and limited" across studies but strong among ASX200 constituents, with a temporary GFC hit.

### Cited Findings
- Hanauer & Windmüller Table 11 (Datastream, 1991-2017, local long-short factors, gross): Australia MOM Sharpe 1.28 (iMOM 1.78); UK 0.79 (iMOM 1.32); Canada 0.71 (iMOM 1.10). For developed markets, "iMOM clearly has the highest or second highest Sharpe ratio for most of the countries". In emerging markets there is "no clear pattern". — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, §4.3 and Table 11](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- Chui, Titman & Wei (2010): momentum is persistent worldwide except in Asia; Rouwenhorst (1998, 1999): momentum earns abnormal returns internationally (both as cited by Hanauer & Windmüller). — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, fn. 1](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf)
- **UK:** Liu, Strong & Xu (1999), weekly UK prices Jan 1977-Dec 1996: momentum profits are not eliminated by controls for systematic risk, size, price, book-to-market or cash-earnings-to-price. Hon & Tonks (2003): momentum profitable 1977-96 but "not present in the earlier 1955-76 period", "implying that momentum is not a general feature of the UK stock market, but is only apparent over certain time periods." — [Hon & Tonks working paper](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/efm/media/workingpapers/working_papers/pdffiles/dp01516.pdf); [FMG record](https://www.fmg.ac.uk/publications/discussion-papers/momentum-uk-stock-market)
- A UK study "Can small investors exploit the momentum effect?" (*Financial Markets and Portfolio Management*, 2009) exists but could not be read (Springer login redirect). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11408-009-0120-3)
- **Australia:** "Existing Australian evidence on the momentum effect is contradictory and limited", partly from differing designs, periods and stock populations, but there is "a strong momentum effect within members of the S&P/ASX200 that was temporarily affected by the GFC". Momentum is most pronounced among winners at longer holding periods. Profits are positive at 3-12-month holding periods and robust to transaction costs and liquidity effects (search summaries of Bangor University and related studies; full texts not read). — [Bangor, "Momentum investing and the GFC: the case of the S&P/ASX100"](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/momentum-investing-and-the-gfc-the-case-of-the-samppasx100/); [Bangor, "Momentum in Australia"](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/momentum-in-australia/); [ResearchGate, "Australian momentum: performance, capacity and the GFC effect"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275673760_Australian_momentum_performance_capacity_and_the_GFC_effect)
- In Australia, short-sale restraints increased momentum profitability but adding bid-ask spreads reduced the gains ("Momentum effect in Australian equities: Revisit, armed with short-selling ban and risk factors", *Pacific-Basin Finance Journal* 2014; abstract-level summary, full text 403). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927538X1400002X)
- Industry- and liquidity-based momentum in Australian equities: strong industry momentum (*Financial Innovation*, open access; not read in full). — [SpringerOpen](https://jfin-swufe.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40854-019-0155-z)
- Winner/loser asymmetry in ASX200 vs S&P500 (title only). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331227195_Modelling_momentum_winnerloser_asymmetry_the_sources_of_winner_and_loser_returns_in_the_ASX200_and_SP500)
- **Canada:** average stock returns relate to past performance, with a short-run momentum effect. Momentum is negatively related to book-to-market and analyst coverage, and size plays no role ("An explanation of momentum in Canadian stocks", search summary). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228958149_An_explanation_of_momentum_in_Canadian_stocks)
- Canadian practitioner screen: buying prior gainers returned 12.7%/yr from end-1999 to September 2025 vs 7.9% for the market (Globe and Mail; costs and survivorship handling unknown). — [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/markets/inside-the-market/article-top-20-canadian-stocks-value-momentum/)
- ASX-listed products exist: the Betashares Australian Momentum ETF (ASX: MTUM, 0.35% fee) and an S&P/ASX 200 Momentum index. — [Betashares](https://www.betashares.com.au/fund/australian-momentum-etf/); [S&P DJI](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/indices/dividends-factors/sp-asx-200-momentum/)

### Inferences
- The strongest country numbers (Australia SR 1.28) are long-short on the full Datastream universe, including small caps, gross. A top-12 long-only ASX sleeve drawn from a large-cap list will not see that figure. The direction (momentum is at least as strong outside the US) is the transferable part.
- Hon & Tonks's 1955-76 null period is a reminder that a single country's momentum premium can vanish for 20 years. That is an argument for the multi-region structure this system already has.
- The ASX MTUM ETF gives a cheap live benchmark for the ASX sleeve: if the sleeve cannot beat it after costs, the sleeve is not adding value.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Canada-specific momentum paper with numbers was read in full; Canadian figures come from Hanauer & Windmüller's table and a newspaper screen.
- No country-specific crash statistics (e.g., Mar-May 2009 for ASX/LSE/TSX) were found.
- Could not access the 2009 "Can small investors exploit the momentum effect?" paper, which is the most retail-relevant UK study.

## 9. Factor crowding, momentum crashes (1932, 2009, 2020) and how institutions hedge them

### Takeaway
The big momentum crashes (1932, March-May 2009, 9 November 2020) all came at sharp market or regime reversals, after which beaten-down losers surged. Institutions manage this mainly by (a) scaling exposure by momentum's own volatility, (b) dynamic scaling in "panic states", (c) residualising the signal and (d) diversifying with value, which is imperfect. Separately, published anomalies lose about a third of their return after publication on average (McLean & Pontiff), and momentum crowding was flagged before the 2020 crash.

### Cited Findings
- 1932: the two worst momentum months were July and August 1932. Losers returned 232% vs winners 32% while the market rose 82%. 2009: March-May, losers +163%, winners +8%, market +26%; at the beginning of March 2009 the loser-decile firms were on average 84% below their peak (Citigroup, Bank of America, Ford, GM). From 8 March 2009 to 28 March 2013 "the losers produce more than twice the" return of winners. — [Daniel & Moskowitz NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- US momentum factor maximum drawdown of -67.10% in 1932 (2x3 construction); global (ex-US) momentum drawdown -36.96% in 2009 (Hanauer & Windmüller). Barroso & Santa-Clara report -96.69% for the decile WML. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft, p.19](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf); [Barroso & Santa-Clara PDF](http://www.snifferquant.com/gyantal/Incode/papers/Momentum%20Has%20Its%20Moments(scaling%20Momentum%20by%20vol),2014.pdf)
- 9 November 2020 (Pfizer vaccine announcement): the momentum factor fell 3.4% in early New York trading, "the biggest intraday drop since records began in 2000", and lost "nearly 5%" on the day, wiping out most of its prior nine months' gains. The MSCI Factor Crowding Model had shown high crowding in momentum since early April 2020, and short interest exposure within the factor was at an all-time high. (Search-summary figures from Bloomberg/MSCI/Omega Point pages; Bloomberg is paywalled and not read directly.) — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-11-09/momentum-trade-plunges-the-most-on-record-in-rotation-frenzy); [MSCI](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/are-momentum-wings-finally-starting-to-melt); [Omega Point](https://www.omegapoint.ai/factor-spotlight-article/the-factors-leading-to-last-weeks-momentum-shock)
- Robeco commentary on momentum after 2020 (title only, not read). — [Robeco, "What's up with Momentum?"](https://www.robeco.com/en-int/insights/2021/01/whats-up-with-momentum)
- Hedging tools and their evidence: vol scaling (section 2), dynamic panic-state scaling (section 2, partly in-sample), residual momentum (section 1, largest drawdown reduction in Hanauer & Windmüller: US max drawdown -67.10% → -25.52%), and value combination (section 4, incomplete hedge per Daniel & Moskowitz). Ex-ante beta hedging fails to mitigate the crash. — [Hanauer & Windmüller 2019 draft](http://wp.lancs.ac.uk/mhf2019/files/2019/09/MHF-2019-076-Matthias-Hanauer.pdf); [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf)
- Post-publication decay: McLean & Pontiff, "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?" (*JF* 71(1), 2016), 97 predictors: portfolio returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication, implying ~32% attributable to publication-informed trading. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v71y2016i1p5-32.html)
- Momentum specifically was persistent in all four 20-year sub-periods of 1927-2013, including the two decades after the original studies (Israel & Moskowitz 2013). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256022216_The_Role_of_Shorting_Firm_Size_and_Time_on_Market_Anomalies)
- Novy-Marx & Velikov: "Transaction costs always reduce strategy profitability, increasing data snooping concerns." — [Novy-Marx & Velikov PDF, abstract](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a long-only book, every crash episode above shows up as **missing the rebound** (winners +8% vs market +26% in Mar-May 2009), not as a portfolio wipe-out. The binary regime filter, if it has the book in cash at the trough, compounds this by also missing the market's +26%. That is the specific cost to measure for this system's filter.
- Crowding indicators (MSCI's model, short-interest within the factor) are institutional data products. A retail proxy, unpublished and untested here, is the valuation spread or return dispersion between the system's own winners and the index.
- McLean & Pontiff's ~58% post-publication haircut is an average across 97 anomalies. Momentum's own record (persistence in post-1993 decades) suggests it has decayed less than average, but post-publication haircuts for residual momentum and vol-scaling specifically have not been published.

### Gaps
- No source was retrieved on the 2022 momentum drawdown specifically; I did not search it, given the tool budget.
- No published per-country crash statistics for ASX/LSE/TSX momentum in 2009 or 2020.
- No institutional "crowding hedge" methodology with out-of-sample evidence was found; the crowding evidence here is descriptive (vendor commentary), not a tested signal.
