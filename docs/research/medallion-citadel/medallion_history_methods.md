## Returns, risk, fees, size cap and the outside-fund contrast

### Takeaway
Per Zuckerman's Appendix 1, reproduced by Cornell (2019), Medallion averaged **66.07% gross / 39.20% net (arithmetic) a year over 1988–2018**, compounding at 63.3% gross. Its gross return was never negative in a calendar year, and its net return was negative only in 1989. That record covers a fund held at about $10bn from 2010, closed to new outside money in 1993 and run for staff from about 2005. The outside funds (RIEF, RIDA and RIDGE) hold positions for months, take far more capital and do far worse: in 2020 Medallion made +76% while RIEF lost about 19–23% and RIDA about 31–34%. **Caveat that must travel with every number:** no audited Medallion series is public, and Cornell's "net" column is computed from a formula rather than taken from what investors actually received.

### Inferences
- [C] **The trade-off between capacity and holding period is the clearest structural lesson.** Same firm, same software, same management (per the investor quote), but two different products:

  | | Medallion | RIEF |
  |---|---|---|
  | Holding period | Days | Months |
  | Size | About $10bn, capped | Targeted at up to $100bn |
  | Leverage | About 12.5x reported | About 2.5x |

  The gap in 2020 and in the long-run record is consistent with short-horizon signals that are large per unit of risk but limited in capacity. Slower signals scale but are crowded and factor-like. This is consistent with Cornell's "scale limit" reading, but it is not proven: leverage, beta target and universe also differ.
- [C] "Never a negative year" is not the same as low risk. On an annual basis the fund looks riskless, yet in August 2007 it lost about 20% in a week. The risk is liquidity and crowding risk in a highly leveraged book, not market beta.
- [C] The beta of about −1 comes from 31 annual points in which the crash years (2000, 2008) were among Medallion's best. It is fragile as an estimate. The robust fact is that the book is long/short and hedged (see Risk management).
- [C] For a retail monthly momentum book, the relevant number is Medallion's **net-of-cost, gross-of-fee** edge, about 66% a year on equity. At about 12.5x leverage that is roughly 5% a year on gross assets: 66 ÷ 12.5 ≈ 5.3%, my arithmetic. A small edge on each dollar is turned into a huge return on equity by leverage and turnover.

### Gaps
- No audited return series is public. I could not see the footnotes to Zuckerman's Appendix 1 that would say where the data came from, so I cannot tell whether its net column is reported or computed. The discrepancies in 1989, 1990 and 2008 are unresolved.
- No public monthly or daily volatility, maximum drawdown or daily Sharpe for Medallion. AIER's claim of "(minor) losses in only one-fifth of the months" has no traceable original source.
- I found no reliable 2021–2025 Medallion returns in this pass. I also found no on-record Renaissance explanation of **why** Medallion is capped at about $10bn.
- The commonly repeated "Medallion pays out all profits every six months" appears only in low-quality secondary posts (e.g. a Substack). It is **not traced** to the book or the press; treat it as unverified.

## Holding period, turnover, trade count and hit rate

### Takeaway
Medallion holds positions for a short time:
- **Early futures era:** about a week or two, then cut to about a day and a half.
- **Equities:** about two days on average.

It makes 150,000–300,000 trades a day, many of them slices of larger orders. The Senate record gives primary-source figures for the equity book held through the bank basket accounts:
- more than 100,000 trades a day, about 30 million a year;
- about 87% of the portfolio turned over within 3 months;
- some positions held only minutes or seconds.

The "50.75%" hit rate is a **Zuckerman-reported** quote from **Robert** Mercer. It is not a disclosed statistic.

### Inferences
- [C] **Breadth is the mechanism.** If 30 million trades a year were independent, the standard error of a hit rate is √(0.25 ÷ 3×10⁷) ≈ 0.009 percentage points. A 0.75 point edge would then be about 80 standard errors. Trades are **not** independent: they are child orders and correlated positions. Even so, many thousands of roughly independent bets a year turn a coin-flip-plus edge into a near-certain annual result. This is the "fundamental law of active management" logic: information ratio ≈ skill per bet × √(number of independent bets).
- [C] "Trade" in the 150k–300k a day and 30 million a year figures counts **executions**, including slices of larger orders. So the denominator of "50.75%" is ambiguous: it is unclear whether it counts per fill, per position or per day.
- [C] For a monthly-rebalanced 12-1 momentum book: holding period is about 1–12 months, the book holds about 10 names, and it trades roughly 12 decision points a year. Its breadth is orders of magnitude smaller, so it cannot rely on the law of large numbers within a year. Its edge has to be larger per bet, and it will be noisy.

## Signal style: where the edge comes from

### Takeaway
The documented building blocks are individually mundane:
- trend-following in futures early on;
- short-term reversion and calendar or intraday seasonality (day of week, overnight versus day sessions, five-minute bars), plus gap reversion;
- pair and lead/lag correlations;
- volatility effects.

Insiders attribute the edge to three things: clean data; rigorous, simple statistics ("simple regression"); and **one unified model** shared by everyone. That model scores each new signal by its marginal value net of cost. Many weak, non-intuitive signals are combined; strong intuitive ones are assumed already traded away.

- [B] Zuckerman ch. 5 p. 97:

- [B] Berlekamp quoted in the book on why to avoid news-driven signals: "I don't deny that earnings reports and other business news surely move markets… The problem is that so many investors focus so much on these types of news that nearly all of their results cluster very near their average." — [bagerbach notes](https://bagerbach.com/books/the-man-who-solved-the-market/)
- [A] Berlekamp, first person: in 1988–89 Ax "resisted my efforts to implement modest improvements in the trading algorithms, favoring instead more work on grander schemes." - [B] Zuckerman quoting **Peter Brown**: "If there were signals that made a lot of sense that were very strong, they would have long-ago been traded out… There are signals that you can't understand, but they're there, and they can be relatively strong."
  - **Note:** the brief attributes this idea to Patterson. The sources I reached attribute this wording to Brown.

- [B] **Robert Mercer** quoted in Sebastian Mallaby's *More Money Than God* (2010): "Some signals that make no intuitive sense do indeed work. … The signals that we have been trading without interruption for fifteen years make no sense. **Brown and Mercer: one unified equity model**
- [A] Brown and Mercer were "two language technology experts from the IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center" hired in 1993 (Bloomberg 2007). — [Bloomberg 2007 mirror](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs224n/cs224n.1114/renaissance.html)
- [B] Zuckerman, via a Shortform summary: the pair were tasked with a single trading code for stocks, "constructing an optimal set of stock holdings given the fund's risk appetite and financial resources." "By combining all their trading signals and portfolio requirements into a single, monolithic model, Renaissance could easily test and add new signals, instantly knowing if the gains from a potential new strategy were likely to top its costs." — [Shortform overview](https://www.shortform.com/blog/the-man-who-solved-the-market-book/)
- [B] The system was "adaptive, or capable of learning and adjusting on its own, much like Henry Laufer's trading system for futures." Brown and Mercer's code was "half million lines." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/); [bagerbach](https://bagerbach.com/books/the-man-who-solved-the-market/)

**No silos, and no overriding the model**
- [A] Simons: "It's an open atmosphere. We make sure everyone knows what everyone else is doing, the sooner the better." — [Scrivner breakdown](https://www.danielscrivner.com/renaissance-technologies-business-breakdown/)
- [A] Peter Brown, Goldman Sachs podcast (11 Sep 2023):
  - "we take a scientific approach to investing and treat the entire problem as a giant problem in mathematics."
  - "Science is best done through collaboration."
  - "we just don't interfere with our trading systems."
  - Paraphrase: the firm has no special economic insight, only the ability to "build large mathematical models."
  — [Marcellus transcript summary](https://marcellus.in/story/a-conversation-with-renaissance-technologies-ceo-peter-brown/)
- [B] Zuckerman ch. 11 pp. 198–199, an insider view: "Any time you hear financial experts talking about how the market went up because of such and such–remember it's all nonsense." — [breakingthemarket Pt II](https://breakingthemarket.com/the-greatest-geometric-balancers-renaissance-technologies-part-ii/)
- [B] Christensen's catalogue of signal families in the book: "Trend, Mean reversion, Pairs, Seasonality, Factors (fundamental), Factors (mathematical), Correlations (lead/lag), Autocorrelation". He adds that the book "delivers no new 'secrets'". — [Christensen](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/review-man-who-solved-market-how-jim-simons-launched-hugh-christensen)

### Inferences
- [C] Mechanism stack, as far as the public record supports it:
  1. Long, clean, intraday data (Straus).
  2. Many small, short-horizon, statistically tested effects (Laufer, Berlekamp).
  3. One model that measures each signal's marginal contribution net of cost against everything else (Brown/Mercer).
  4. Bet sizing by expected edge, Kelly-style (Laufer, Berlekamp).
  5. Cost-aware execution (next section).
  6. Leverage (later section).

  No single exotic signal is documented.
- [C] On Brown's and Mercer's own logic, a well-known, "sensible" signal is the least likely place for an anomaly. 12-1 momentum is the textbook example. A retail momentum book is harvesting a published premium, not a Medallion-style anomaly.
- [C] The unified model is mainly about **governance**. It stops two teams' signals from trading against each other and charging double costs. It also forces every new idea to prove its value net of costs at the margin, which a collection of separate strategies cannot do.

### Gaps
- I could not locate Zuckerman's exact "24-hour effect" wording, or a description of it, in any accessible source. The day-of-week finding above is confirmed; the specific term is **not verified** here.
- No source shows hidden Markov models used **in Medallion's production trading**. Baum's role is documented only for the Monemetrics currency era. "HMMs power Medallion" is speculation [C].
- Number of signals, form of the combiner (regression versus other machine learning) and retraining cadence: not public.

## Costs, execution and sim-vs-live divergence

### Takeaway
Cost and market-impact control was an early, explicit worry and a design driver:
- Ax resisted short-term trading because of commissions and slippage he could not measure.
- Orders were spread through the day toward high-volume periods.
- Equity orders were sliced into small pieces.
- The unified model gated new signals on beating their costs.

The best documented gap between simulation and live trading is Brown and Mercer's mid-1990s stock system. Its profits "evaporated" as size grew until David Magerman found bugs, including a hard-coded S&P 500 level.

### Cited Findings
**Early cost concerns (Zuckerman)**
- [B] "Ax had resisted shifting to a more frequent, short-term trading strategy, partly because he worried brokerage commissions and other costs… would offset possible profits." — [twoquants Pt 1](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-1/)
- [B] Ax "had also been concerned that rapid trading would push prices… a cost called slippage, which Medallion couldn't measure with any accuracy." — [twoquants Pt 1](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-1/)
- [B] "Profitable trade ideas are only half the game; the act of buying and selling investments can itself affect prices to such a degree that gains can be whittled away." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/)

**How orders were worked (Zuckerman)**
- [B] Orders were "sent to a team of traders five times a day", later "increased to sixteen times a day, reducing the impact on prices by focusing on the periods when there was the most volume." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/)
- [B] Renaissance developed "sophisticated approaches to direct trades to various futures exchanges to reduce the market impact of each trade." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/)
- [B] In equities, 150,000–300,000 trades a day, "in small chunks to avoid impacting the market prices." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/)
- [B] The monolithic model meant "instantly knowing if the gains from a potential new strategy were likely to top its costs." — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/the-man-who-solved-the-market-book/)

**The sim-vs-live failure and the Magerman bug**
- [B] Zuckerman ch. 10 p. 194: "when Simons fed it leverage and the trades got bigger, profits evaporated." — [breakingthemarket Pt II](https://breakingthemarket.com/the-greatest-geometric-balancers-renaissance-technologies-part-ii/)
- [B] Secondary summaries of the same episode:
  - The new stock system "couldn't handle much money". With about $35m placed, gains dissipated as more was traded.
  - David Magerman found that "a line of code used a static figure for the Standard & Poor's 500 from 1991 that was roughly half the current number, rather than a variable that updated."
  - He "found and fixed crucial bugs in Brown and Mercer's code", after which the system worked.
  - I saw this through search summaries and notes sites, not the book text.
  — [bagerbach](https://bagerbach.com/books/the-man-who-solved-the-market/); search summary of [Medium review](https://medium.com/@ilyakavalerov/the-man-who-solved-the-market-and-the-solution-was-hmms-and-regression-dd60cea5a6d7)

**Cornell's view of costs**
- [A] Cornell: the gross returns are "after trading costs… It also implies that Renaissance was apparently particularly effective in minimizing such costs." — [Cornell PDF](https://community.portfolio123.com/uploads/short-url/wZJco8G9Xd3diZqEN2YNc3RdaH.pdf)

**Execution through the banks (Senate record)**
- [A] Levin, describing the Barclays "Palomino" accounts: RenTec "was charged commissions and trading costs by Barclays for each of the transactions", paid "finance fees… to borrow shares for its short selling", paid "a financing fee… on the amount that it used for leverage", and "even received the rebates from stock exchanges for the orders using Barclays' execution system." Barclays' witness answered: "In general, yes." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Deutsche Bank's witness: Renaissance's orders "were sent through our slower system initially, which was in the range of a few milliseconds." Renaissance "has direct access to Barclays' and Deutsche Bank's trading platforms to place orders." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)

**Investment in research and computing**
- [A] Silber, Renaissance's CFO, under oath: "We have invested billions in the research that supports our models… Our equity trading and advisory activities add liquidity to the markets." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Bloomberg 2007: "From 1994 to 2000, Renaissance's total CPU power grew by a factor of 50. Data bandwidth in and out of Renaissance headquarters rose by a factor of 45." — [Bloomberg 2007 mirror](https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs224n/cs224n.1114/renaissance.html)

### Inferences
- [C] Exchange rebates, sliced orders, "add liquidity" language and Cornell's "better market maker" hypothesis together suggest part of Medallion's edge is **earned in execution**: capturing the bid-ask spread and avoiding impact. That part is invisible in a close-to-close backtest.
- [C] The Magerman episode is the canonical lesson: a model can be profitable in simulation at small size and lose money live once size grows. The cause can be impact or a mundane code error, and both look the same in live P&L. Medallion found it by digging into why live and simulated results diverged.
- [C] For a retail monthly book, costs per trade are small relative to monthly signal moves. The larger risks are model or data bugs and drift between simulated and live fills, the class of issue `verify.py` already audits.

### Gaps
- No public figure for Medallion's cost per share or basis point, its impact model, or the share of gross P&L consumed by costs.
- No dated, primary account of the Magerman bug. The exact chronology (circa 1995–96) and the second bug's details are not verified here.
- No public figure for spending on execution research specifically, as opposed to "billions in research" overall.

## Leverage and financing: basket options, the 2014 Senate report, the 2021 IRS settlement

### Takeaway
Primary Senate testimony documents leverage of up to **20:1** through Deutsche Bank and Barclays basket options:
- Renaissance put up 10% and the bank lent 90%.
- A knock-out barrier liquidated the account at a 10% loss.

Those options were held about 400–450 days to convert short-term trading gains into long-term capital gains: about $34bn of pre-tax profit and $6–6.8bn of tax, by the Senate's estimate. The often-quoted "12.5x average, up to 20x" leverage comes from Zuckerman, but I could not locate the exact passage. In 2021 Renaissance executives settled with the IRS for **up to about $7bn**, covering Medallion trades from 2005 to 2015.

### Cited Findings
**How the basket options worked (Senate record, 22 July 2014)**
- [A] Levin: "The hedge fund put up 10 percent of the cash needed to buy the securities, and the bank lent the other 90 percent." Normal brokerage margin rules imply 2:1, but "the banks offered their hedge fund clients leverage as high as 20:1. RenTec used the increased leverage to borrow billions of dollars." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Rosenthal's worked mechanics:
  - "a hedge fund might deposit, say, $10 million. The bank also might contribute $90 million, which permitted up to $100 million to trade."
  - Contracts "typically had a term of 2 or 3 years".
  - The account "would automatically be knocked out—that is, liquidated—if the value of the account fell from $100 million to $90 million… In practice, no basket contract was knocked out, none of the 60."
  — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Deutsche Bank internal emails entered as exhibits:
  - August 2009: "If client started the day with maximum leverage (it has never done so), longs would have to underperform shorts by 11% to burn through capital… We have triggers in place that allow us to sieze control of the portfolio at any point during the day if half of the capital is depleted (ie, 5.5% long underperformance of shorts)."
  - November 2009: "they were targeting the 7X Init Leverage… We either need 8.45X Init Leverage or Libor + 133bps."
  - June 2008: a "33bn GMV [Gross Market Value] threshold."
  - June 2008: a RenTec/DB suggestion to "stagger options once every 3 months."
  — [govinfo exhibit list](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] **Renaissance's own explanation** (Mark Silber, CFO/General Counsel, under oath):
  - "Medallion purchases barrier options to obtain a combination of leverage and loss protection that we have been unable to obtain through any other means."
  - Options were held on average "around 450 days" for Deutsche Bank (2000–2009) and "around 400 days" for Barclays.
  - "After the August 2007 Quant Quake, we exercised a Barclays option after only a few months, and then did so again in 2009."
  - On counterparty risk: "we would be a general creditor at the back of the line."
  - By 2014, "all of the new options that Medallion enters into have terms of less than 1 year."
  — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)

**The scale of the profits and the tax**
- [A] Sen. McCain: "between the years 2000 and 2014, Renaissance exercised 60 long-term basket options with Deutsche Bank and Barclays, earning in the neighborhood of $34 billion in pre-tax profits and potentially avoiding over $6 billion in taxes." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Rosenthal: the structure "purported to reduce the tax rate on the gains from 35 percent to 15 percent and reduced taxes paid to the Treasury by approximately $6.8 billion." Levin: the staff estimate, "based on basket option profits that RenTec reported from 2000 to 2013," was "more than $6 billion." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Bloomberg via Accounting Today (22 July 2014):
  - The banks sold 199 basket options since 1999, 127 of them structured for possible long-term treatment.
  - The banks earned about $1.1bn.
  - Peter Brown said the options provided "precisely the protection we need."
  — [Accounting Today](https://www.accountingtoday.com/news/renaissance-avoided-more-than-6-billion-tax-senators-say)
- [A] Timeline (Levin):
  - 2010: the IRS warned that these options could not claim long-term rates.
  - Barclays continued selling until revising its product in 2013.
  - Deutsche Bank suspended new issuance after 2010, then resumed in 2012 with terms of less than a year.
  — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)

**Leverage figures from Zuckerman**
- [B] "When Medallion spied especially juicy opportunities, such as during a 2002 market downturn, the fund could boost its leverage, holding close to $20 of assets for each dollar of cash." — [twoquants Pt 2](https://twoquants.com/the-man-who-solved-the-market-part-2/)
- [B, exact passage not located] "Medallion's leverage has averaged 12.5 times its equity and has occasionally zoomed as high as 20 times." This is widely attributed to Zuckerman. Leithner wrongly attributes it to "Simons' article", and is actually quoting a QuantifiedStrategies post dated 26 Jan 2025. — [Leithner](https://www.chrisleithner.com/how-warren-buffett-has-trounced-the-worlds-greatest-hedge-fund-manager/); [QuantifiedStrategies](https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/risk-and-reward-how-leverage-amplified-the-medallion-funds-gains/)

**The 2021 IRS settlement (Reuters, 2–3 Sept 2021)**
- [A] Executives "could pay as much as $7 billion".
- [A] "The trades in question were done by its flagship Medallion fund between 2005 and 2015."
- [A] "The seven individuals who were members of Renaissance's board during those years, and their spouses, will be required to pay the tax and interest and penalties. Investors in the fund will be required to pay additional tax and interest but not penalties."
- [A] Simons "will make an additional settlement payment of $670 million."
- [A] Peter Brown's letter to Medallion investors: settle "rather than risking a worse outcome, including harsher terms and penalties, that could result from litigation."
- [A] Long Island Press (6 Sept 2021) puts the total at "about $6.8 billion".
— [Reuters via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/renaissance-executives-pay-7-billion-231750167.html); [Long Island Press](https://www.longislandpress.com/2021/09/06/how-long-island-execs-just-settled-the-biggest-tax-case-in-u-s-history/)

### Inferences
- [C] The structure gave three things at once:
  1. Leverage far beyond the 2:1 margin limit (about 7–8.45x initial in the late-2009 DB emails, up to 20:1 in the Senate's framing).
  2. A cap on loss per option (the barrier works like a bought put).
  3. A tax-rate arbitrage. Cutting the rate from 35% to 15% more than halved the tax per dollar of profit and raised the after-tax share investors kept by about 31%: (1 − 0.15) ÷ (1 − 0.35) ≈ 1.31x.

  Part of Medallion's historical *after-tax* economics was therefore a financing and tax structure, not signal alpha. That part was unwound after 2010–2013 and settled in 2021.
- [C] The DB triggers show how much room a 7–10x long/short book has. A 5.5% underperformance of longs versus shorts in a day halves the capital and lets the bank take control. That buffer is only survivable if the book is tightly hedged, with longs and shorts matched.
- [C] Leverage of this kind is not available to a retail account. US margin rules allow about 2:1 overnight and 4:1 intraday for pattern day traders. Australian retail CFDs and margin loans carry different and costly terms. Any gap analysis should treat leverage as structurally unavailable rather than a missing feature.

### Gaps
- The full 2014 PSI staff report (hsgac.senate.gov PDF) was blocked (HTTP 403). Report-only tables, such as per-option profit and a leverage history, were not read. The report is said to contain more on leverage and holding periods.
- No public leverage time series. It is unclear whether "12.5x" is gross or net exposure, or whether it covers futures. I did not locate the exact 12.5x passage in Zuckerman.
- The financing structure after 2015 is not public.

## Risk management: position limits, market neutrality, drawdowns

### Takeaway
The public record shows:
- a **long/short, roughly market-neutral equity book** (implied by the DB "longs vs shorts" trigger and a negative measured beta);
- **automatic de-risking** when a strategy fails or volatility spikes;
- a **guideline-bound universe** of about 6,000 securities;
- **counterparty-imposed hard limits**: a 10% knock-out and intraday seizure at a 5.5% long-versus-short loss.

No numeric internal position limits, VaR or drawdown rules are public. August 2007, a fall of about 20% in a week, is the only well-documented severe drawdown.

### Cited Findings
- [B] Zuckerman ch. 11 p. 213: "If a strategy wasn't working, or when market volatility surged, Renaissance's system tended to automatically reduce positions and risk." — [breakingthemarket Pt II](https://breakingthemarket.com/the-greatest-geometric-balancers-renaissance-technologies-part-ii/)
- [A] DB email (Aug 2009) describing RenTec's book in long-versus-short terms: "longs would have to underperform shorts by 11% to burn through capital… triggers… if half of the capital is depleted (ie, 5.5% long underperformance of shorts)." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Trading inside the bank accounts was bound by agreed "guidelines". Barclays' Malloy: "If it was inside the investment guidelines…". The algorithm picked among about 6,000 securities. The banks had a legal right to intervene but "did not exercise it until after the fact in many instances" (Malloy). — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Rosenthal: the barrier knocked out at a 10% loss of account value; "the banks also put in place protections to prevent the account from falling that much. In practice, no basket contract was knocked out." — [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Cornell: beta of about −1.0 against the CRSP market index on annual data, with negative but insignificant SMB and HML loadings. — [Cornell PDF](https://community.portfolio123.com/uploads/short-url/wZJco8G9Xd3diZqEN2YNc3RdaH.pdf)
- [B] August 2007: Medallion lost more than $1bn, about 20%, in the week to 9 August. Simons "didn't know if his firm could survive much more pain" (Zuckerman, via Leithner). A search summary of the book says Simons decided at an Aug 6 meeting to sell positions until the market stabilized; I did not verify this against the book text. Silber's testimony confirms a Barclays option was exercised early, "after only a few months," after the Quant Quake. — [Leithner](https://www.chrisleithner.com/how-warren-buffett-has-trounced-the-worlds-greatest-hedge-fund-manager/); [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [A] Brown (2023): "we just don't interfere with our trading systems." Silber (2014): "past success does not eliminate future risk." — [Marcellus](https://marcellus.in/story/a-conversation-with-renaissance-technologies-ceo-peter-brown/); [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-113shrg89882/html/CHRG-113shrg89882.htm)
- [B] Patterson, in Zuckerman: "We never believed our models reflected reality—just some aspects." This is the cultural basis for de-risking rather than doubling down. — [bagerbach](https://bagerbach.com/books/the-man-who-solved-the-market/)

### Inferences
- [C] The main risk in this kind of book is crowded unwinds (2007), not market direction. The defence is a hedged book, automatic risk reduction when volatility rises, and lenders that cap the loss per account.
- [C] There is tension between "we don't interfere" (Brown, 2023) and the reported 2007 decision to cut positions. It is possible Renaissance treats a risk-level cut as distinct from overriding signals. That is my inference, not documented.
- [C] For a long-only or 130/30-style retail momentum book, the analogue is a volatility-triggered de-gross plus a drawdown breaker. The repo already has a breaker (per CLAUDE.md). Medallion's version is continuous and automatic, not a single threshold.

## Asset classes over time

### Takeaway
The sequence was:
1. Currencies at Monemetrics (1978, Baum).
2. Diversified futures at Axcom/Medallion from 1988: commodities, currencies, bonds and stock-index futures, with trend and then short-term effects.
3. After Brown and Mercer arrived in 1993, a unified **equity statistical-arbitrage** system, which took off in the mid-1990s.
4. By the 2000s, a heavily leveraged equity book of about 6,000 names run through bank basket options.

Medallion is reported to trade "Everything". RIEF (2005) is a separate, slower, long-biased US equity product.
